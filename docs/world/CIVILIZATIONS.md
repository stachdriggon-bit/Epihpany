# EPIPHANY — Civilizations and Societies

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Design framework with provisional regional patterns; no complete cultures or states are finalized.

## 1. Design principles

- No single civilization is the origin of all peoples.
- Societies may develop independently and later exchange technologies, words, beliefs, art, or institutions.
- Cultural influence does not automatically prove common ancestry.
- Regions contain internal diversity, rivalries, minorities, and changing identities.
- Avoid treating geography as destiny or reducing a people to one trait.
- Political borders and cultural regions need not coincide.
- A state's military defeat does not erase its cultural, linguistic, legal, or technological legacy.

## 2. Provisional regional patterns

These are prompts for future development, not stereotypes or finalized canon.

### Ilan river-basin societies
Potential themes: water management, agriculture, river transport, urban centers, disputes over allocation and maintenance.

### Orath highland and pass societies
Potential themes: local route and weather knowledge, separated basins, tolls and transit, exchange of knowledge versus strategic secrecy.

### Tharima basin and caravan societies
Potential themes: water infrastructure, seasonal movement, caravan logistics, unequal access to resources, negotiation among settlements.

### Nirvan coastal communities
Potential themes: ports, fisheries, forests, minerals, seasonal navigation, autonomy versus outside connections.

### Azmar maritime cities
Potential themes: navigation, shipbuilding, trade, mediation, competition between ports, dependence on sea routes.

### Forest communities across regions
Potential themes: varied livelihoods and environmental knowledge. Forest societies are not one people, one government, or one belief system.

## 3. Civilization/state record template

For each society, record:
- Stable ID and names in relevant languages.
- Region and settlement pattern.
- Languages, identities, and internal diversity.
- Food systems, resources, infrastructure, and technologies.
- Institutions, authority, law, and succession.
- Trade, migration, and diplomatic connections.
- Relations with neighboring societies.
- Beliefs, art, education, and knowledge traditions.
- Conflicts and internal disagreements.
- Historical changes and legacy.
- Evidence, disputed accounts, and status.
- Associated stories, characters, places, and cards.

## 4. Historical balance

Do not repeat a simple pattern of a great civilization rising, collapsing, and being replaced by a greater one. Power can shift gradually; states can persist while losing influence; local communities can outlast empires; a conquered people can reshape its conquerors; and ordinary institutions can matter more than spectacular rulers.

## 5. Open questions

Specific names, languages, political systems, religions, technologies, state borders, dates, and inter-civilization relationships remain open until developed and recorded.
