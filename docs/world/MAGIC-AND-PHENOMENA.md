# EPIPHANY — Magic and Unusual Phenomena Framework

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Foundational design framework; specific magic mechanics remain open.  
**Purpose:** Make EPIPHANY unmistakably fantasy and magical while preserving continuity, evidence, regional variety, and room for future stories. This document is documentation only; it does not change the app runtime.

## 1. What is established, and what is not

### Established direction
- EPIPHANY is a fantasy setting. Magic and wondrous phenomena belong in the world; they should not be reduced to occasional decorative effects.
- Extraordinary phenomena exist alongside recognizable geography, work, family, trade, institutions, humor, conflict, and ordinary decisions.
- Phenomena vary by place, scale, danger, and how well people understand them.
- Some characters can interact with or use magic, while others cannot. Magical ability is not universal; define each character's specific access, method, knowledge, and limits rather than assuming every person can cast or control magic.
- No universal magic region, single origin for every phenomenon, or one master explanation for all mysteries is assumed.
- The world's ultimate origin remains unresolved.
- Myths, official records, witness accounts, and expert interpretations are not automatically objective truth.

### Still open
The current references do **not** yet define a complete magic system. Do not assume any of the following without a separate decision:
- a universal source or energy of magic;
- the complete rules of magical access: which people can use it, whether ability can be learned, inherited, borrowed, or accessed by non-human beings, and how any such paths work. It is established that not every character can use magic;
- costs, exhaustion, materials, gestures, words, symbols, or other requirements;
- whether magic can heal, revive, transform, predict, teleport, alter memory, or change time;
- a fixed taxonomy of magical schools;
- a universal religion, creator, ancient magical civilization, or hidden force behind all phenomena;
- the full range, nature, intelligence, or origins of fantastic creatures.

These are design questions, not missing facts to fill casually while writing a scene.

## 2. The fantasy promise

A reader should sometimes encounter something genuinely wondrous, not merely an ordinary problem with a strange label. Magic can shape how people travel, build, work, communicate, protect resources, celebrate, remember, or understand their surroundings. It can also be beautiful, funny, inconvenient, comforting, dangerous, or misunderstood.

Not every story needs a threat or a grand revelation. A magical setting becomes convincing when extraordinary things have ordinary consequences: someone maintains a device, a community sets fair access rules, a craft develops around a local property, a child misunderstands a custom, or two competent people disagree about what the evidence means.

## 3. Design lenses for phenomena

These are **organizing lenses**, not canon categories or named magical schools. A phenomenon may fit more than one.

- **Place-bound:** linked to a particular landscape, water source, pass, forest, island, or built environment.
- **Living:** associated with a plant, animal, creature, person, or ecological relationship.
- **Made or maintained:** produced, shaped, stored, or regulated by craft, tools, architecture, or collective upkeep.
- **Perceptual or informational:** affects what can be sensed, recorded, interpreted, or communicated. Take special care not to use this category as an excuse to erase evidence or solve every mystery.
- **Cyclical or conditional:** appears only under certain environmental, seasonal, astronomical, social, or other conditions. Conditions must be defined for each phenomenon rather than assumed universal.
- **Rare or poorly understood:** documented irregularly or known through conflicting evidence. “Unexplained” describes the state of knowledge; it is not itself a cause.

Use these lenses to help generate varied stories. Do not force every phenomenon into one category.

## 4. A record template for each significant magical phenomenon

Before a recurring or consequential phenomenon becomes established lore, record:

1. **Stable ID and working name**
2. **Status:** accepted, provisional, in-world claim, disputed, or open
3. **Where it occurs:** precise place ID or explicitly broad distribution
4. **Observable effect:** what witnesses can actually see, hear, measure, or experience
5. **Conditions:** when it occurs and what has been observed to change it
6. **Limits:** what it cannot do, where it stops, or what it does not reliably affect
7. **Costs and risks:** physical, social, material, ecological, ethical, or other consequences, if any
8. **People and knowledge:** who understands it, who uses it, who disagrees, and how that knowledge was acquired
9. **Daily-life consequences:** work, travel, care, trade, custom, law, access, maintenance, or inequality
10. **Evidence and interpretations:** direct observations separated from hypotheses, folklore, mistakes, and propaganda
11. **History:** what is known about its local history; leave dates and origin open when unsupported
12. **Story utility:** what kinds of choices or conflicts it can create without automatically resolving them
13. **Continuity links:** affected places, characters, institutions, events, maps, cards, and stories

A phenomenon does not need every answer before it can appear in a small story. But the more often it recurs, the greater its consequences, or the more power it gives a character, the more clearly its rules and limits should be recorded.

## 5. Rules for keeping magic coherent

- **Show before explaining:** establish what happens in observable terms before giving the reader a theory.
- **Define the scope:** a local effect should not silently become a continent-wide or world-wide rule.
- **Make limits matter:** if a capability solves a problem, explain why it is available here, what it can affect, and why it does not effortlessly solve every related problem.
- **Keep consequences real:** magic may change a problem, but it should not erase human choices, resource limits, geography, or social disagreement.
- **No automatic cost formula:** some magic may be tiring or costly; other phenomena may be passive or harmless. Decide per phenomenon rather than making one universal rule without approval.
- **No universal explanation:** two similar effects may share a cause, differ in cause, or only look similar. Establish the connection with evidence.
- **Experts can be competent and still disagree:** they may use different evidence, methods, priorities, or definitions.
- **Folklore can matter without being literally true:** a belief may shape behavior, rituals, laws, fear, or identity even when its explanation is mistaken.
- **Do not make every mystery supernatural:** mundane causes, mixed causes, errors, missing records, and unresolved alternatives remain possible.
- **Do not make every supernatural event a mystery:** some magical practices can be familiar, reliable, and well understood by the people who use them.
- **Protect existing open questions:** in particular, do not retroactively make the Low-Water Shore pilot proof of a supernatural lake or a hidden world-scale cause.

## 7. Creatures and non-human life

The current reviewed references do not establish a complete creature catalogue or universal rules for non-human intelligence. Before adding a recurring creature species, decide only what the story needs and record:
- what observers can reliably see;
- habitat, food, movement, and environmental role where relevant;
- whether it is intelligent, social, trainable, dangerous, magical, or simply unfamiliar — do not assume these traits from appearance;
- how people interact with it and what the creature can refuse, resist, or do independently;
- limits, needs, vulnerabilities, and the difference between a documented trait and a local belief;
- whether it is a unique individual, a local population, or a widespread species.

Avoid making all unusual creatures monsters, pets, wise guides, or collectible rewards. Their place in the world should be broader than one gameplay function.

## 8. How magic becomes history

Record an event in local history when a phenomenon causes a meaningful, evidenced change — for example, a settlement changes its maintenance practice, a route becomes seasonal, a craft develops, or a community revises an agreement. A phenomenon's mere existence does not prove that it caused a historical change.

When connecting regions, state the transmission mechanism: travel, trade, migration, shared training, copied records, political contact, or a documented environmental effect. Similar appearances alone do not prove common origin.

For every magical historical claim, keep separate:
- when the event occurred, if known;
- when someone recorded or understood it;
- what evidence survives;
- which explanation is accepted, disputed, or open.

Do not add exact dates merely to populate a timeline.

## 9. Suggested workflow

1. Keep this framework as the shared standard.
2. Review any existing project material that explicitly defines magic, creatures, powers, or supernatural rules before adding mechanics.
3. Create a small number of varied phenomenon records with clear status labels and local scope.
4. Test each against geography, daily life, limitations, and story potential.
5. Write one local story only after its magical premise has an internally consistent behavior and limit.
6. Add event IDs and timeline links after the story text is settled.
7. Promote a proposal to accepted canon only through an explicit owner decision and the Decision Log.
