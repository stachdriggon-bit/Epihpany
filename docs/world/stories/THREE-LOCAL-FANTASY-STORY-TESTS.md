# EPIPHANY — Three Local Fantasy Story Tests

**Version:** 0.4  
**Status:** All three stories approved as local story-scene canon by the project owner; not independently verified history. Events are registered undated in `EVENT-REGISTRY.md`.  
**Purpose:** Test three distinct magical phenomena through vivid local fantasy, memorable characters, and different kinds of story tension: immediate danger, patient investigation, and a community under pressure. No global calendar dates are assigned. These drafts are not registered as historical events; event IDs should be added only after story approval.

## Revision notes

The project owner approved all three stories. Before approval, the third story received a clarity edit: the stormglass returns only a faint echo resembling the last filling storm, not a complete recording. The first story is driven by immediate danger, the second by observation and a thread seeker's fear of being wrong, and the third by a shared resource crisis. Small local practices remain story-specific details, not universal customs. The magic stays bounded: it changes what characters notice and must respond to, but does not solve every practical or social problem.

## 1. Seven Breaths Beneath the Middle Arch

**Place:** IL-LOC-01 — The Three-Arch Ferry  
**Phenomenon:** MP-IL-001 — The Returning Current  
**Story focus:** A dangerous crossing, a practical guide, and who should pay to keep a shared route safe

The market bells were still ringing when the ferry rope burned across Mira's palms.

“Not that knot!” she shouted. “The wet one—pull the wet one!”

The boat had drifted halfway beneath the three arches. Then the water under the middle arch began to move the wrong way.

It was only a narrow ribbon, no wider than a cart track, but it shone against the dusk as if someone had drawn a strip of black glass through the river. Reeds bent upstream inside it. Outside that strip, the river hurried toward the sea as it always had.

The rope gave a sharp sideways jerk.

Sacks of flour slid across the deck. A little boy dropped his wooden fish, and it skittered toward the edge. Mira caught the child by his collar while the ferryman planted both boots against the landing post.

“One,” the ferryman counted through his teeth.

The ribbon kept running backward.

“Two. Three.”

The boat swung hard enough to throw the ferryman to one knee. The boy clung to Mira's sleeve. Nobody counted the remaining breaths. They were too busy holding on.

When the strange current vanished, the ferry struck the landing with a crack that split a crate and sent apples rolling between everyone's feet. No one had gone overboard. The wooden fish, however, was gone.

By morning, the story had grown larger than the river. A trader said the water had warned them of a coming storm. A mill worker swore it always appeared before a bad harvest. The ferryman listened, then held up the frayed rope.

“This warned me,” he said. “For a month.”

The bridge keeper wanted a toll from every traveler. The arches needed inspection, the landing post was loose, and the ferry's anchor ring had worn thin. The keeper spoke as if the river itself had presented him with a bill.

Mira folded her blistered hands. “People on foot use this crossing to reach the market. Some carry nothing but a basket. Charge them, and you'll collect the most from the people with the least.”

“And if the rope breaks?” the keeper asked.

“Then the river won't care who paid.”

That silenced the meeting for a moment.

Mira did not know why the current had reversed. She knew only how the boat had moved when the rope slackened. She persuaded the keeper to inspect the anchor points before they argued any further about the river's intentions.

The inspection found a worn iron ring and a split timber brace. Neither explained the backward ribbon of water, but both could have turned a strange moment into a drowning.

The keeper returned to the market the next day with the repairer's estimate written on a board: the ring and brace first, the landing post after that. He proposed a levy on every traveler until the costs were covered. A porter asked what would happen to people who crossed on foot to buy a single loaf; a trader asked whether loaded carts would pay the same as empty ones. The argument lasted longer than the inspection had.

Mira suggested a shorter trial: only loaded commercial carts would contribute, the repair costs and every payment would be posted publicly, and the rule would end when the repairs were paid for. Foot travelers would pay nothing. The keeper objected that the amount might not be enough. The ferryman pointed out that losing the crossing would cost everyone more, but warned that the levy must not become permanent by neglect.

They agreed to try it, then review the tally together after the first week and again when the work was complete. It was not a perfect answer, and nobody mistook it for one. The keeper still worried about the shortfall; the ferryman still grumbled about the ring. For once, both complaints would have to meet the same public record.

For the next few evenings, Mira tied a short pale thread around the ferry rope before dusk. It was not a charm, she told the children who asked. It made fraying easier to see in the fading light.

The river flowed forward the next evening. Mira watched the middle arch anyway.

The current had given them a reason to look closely. It had not told them what a fair crossing should cost.

**What this draft establishes provisionally**
- The Returning Current is visible and physically hazardous, but confined to a narrow ribbon beneath the middle arch.
- Witnesses turn an unexplained wonder into competing local beliefs.
- Mira's practical knowledge helps the community act without pretending to explain the magic.
- The pale thread on the ferry rope is a proposed practical habit, not a magical protection.

**What remains open**
- Why and how often the current appears.
- Whether the temporary levy survives.
- Whether the rope-thread practice becomes a lasting local custom.
- Any exact date, origin story, or connection to other phenomena.

## 2. The Thread That Climbed

**Place:** TH-LOC-02 — The Spring Steps  
**Phenomenon:** MP-TH-001 — The Climbing Thread  
**Story focus:** Evin the Thread Seeker, a strange sign that cannot prove guilt, and a water-sharing agreement under pressure

The upper terraces had been thirsty for three weeks.

By noon, the last garden beds were dust. The channels still carried water, but less of it reached the upper steps, and every household had a different explanation. Someone had planted too much. Someone had taken an extra turn. Someone had neglected the channel after promising to mend it.

At dusk, Evin knelt beside the carved groove on the lowest terrace. He had spent only one season helping care for the channels, and he worried that the older repairers dismissed his observations too quickly. As the Thread Seeker, he had watched the Climbing Thread—a line of water as fine as a hair, sometimes rising up the groove before slipping back down—but this was the first dry season in which people expected him to explain what it meant.

The air smelled of warm stone and crushed mint. A few drops gathered in the groove. They trembled, then crept uphill.

Evin leaned closer.

The thread reached the second mark and stopped. A silver bead quivered at its tip before sliding down into the channel.

“Not high enough,” said a gardener behind him. “Someone has taken more than their share.”

Evin looked toward the upper beds. Their leaves hung like folded paper. He wanted the gardener to be right. The maintenance rota had been ignored twice, and the same few households seemed always to be repairing the channels.

But nobody had measured the thread on earlier evenings. It had never been a measuring cup. It could not tell them how much water had passed through every terrace.

“Then let's look at the channel,” Evin said. “Not at our neighbors.”

They followed it by lantern light. At the third terrace, the water made a thin, hollow sound behind a stone lip. An older repairer pressed his ear to the wall, then tapped the stone with the wooden handle of his tool.

“Something is choking the side passage.”

They lifted a loose cover stone. Roots had pushed into the channel, and silt had packed around them until the opening was little more than a dark slit. The roots came from a hardy plant growing beside the wall. No one had cut them there deliberately.

They worked until their hands were muddy and the lantern smoked. When the passage opened, water slipped through with a low, bright trickle. It was not enough to fill the upper beds, but it reached farther than before. For a moment, the thin stream caught the lantern light and seemed to climb the stone itself. Evin blinked; it had already settled into its ordinary course.

Evin marked the damaged stone with a small chalk line and wrote down what they had found. His hand hovered over the page before he added what they had *not* found: no proof that anyone had stolen water, and no answer to why the spring itself was weaker. The older repairer read the entry and nodded. He did not praise Evin, but he did not correct the record either.

The next evening, the Climbing Thread rose a little higher. It wavered in the groove like a tiny silver vine. Then it fell back.

Evin felt the old urge to call it a good sign. Instead, he wrote down how far it had climbed and checked the channel marks. The upper terraces were still short of water.

At the meeting, one gardener demanded that the old schedule be enforced exactly. Another pointed out that the schedule had been written when fewer families lived above the steps. The repairer asked why the households who had done the most recent work should be expected to do it all again.

They argued until the lanterns burned low. In the end, they agreed to repair the cracked channel, record the flow at the same marked points each day, and rotate water shares until they understood the shortage better. The schedule would be posted where everyone could see it. Repair duties would rotate too.

Evin returned to the groove at dusk. The thread rose, bright against the darkening stone, and stopped below the highest mark.

He recorded that too.

The wonder had not measured the water for them. It had reminded them to pay attention—and left them with the harder work of deciding what to do.

**What this draft establishes provisionally**
- The Climbing Thread is a brief, delicate magical effect, not a source of extra water.
- A physical obstruction is found, but it does not fully explain the reduced spring flow.
- Evin chooses inspection over accusation and records both evidence and uncertainty.
- The chalk mark and shared flow record are proposed local practices, not magical tools.

**What remains open**
- The phenomenon's conditions and cause.
- The reason the spring flow weakened.
- Whether the new sharing and repair schedule lasts.
- Whether the community develops a consistent way to record the thread without treating it as an exact gauge.

## 3. When the Stormglass Fell Silent

**Place:** AZ-LOC-02 — The Rain-Cistern Island  
**Phenomenon:** MP-AZ-001 — The Stormglass Echo  
**Story focus:** A familiar magical sound disappears during a period of scarce water

The cistern had a voice, though it never spoke a word. Evren, a resonance tinkerer, had been helping the keeper inspect the fitting, but neither of them treated its sound as a precise measure.

When the keeper tapped the small grey stone fitted into its wall, a faint echo answered, resembling the storm that had last filled the cistern—not a complete recording, only a trace held in the thumb-sized stone. As the water sank lower, the echo tended to change pitch, though no one could turn a note into an exact volume. Children tried to hum it. Boat crews listened before taking on supplies. The keepers treated it as a rough clue and checked it against their marked measuring rod.

They called the fitting stormglass, even though it was stone, rough at the edges and cool as shade.

One morning, the keeper tapped it and heard nothing.

He tried again, more gently. A dry click came from the cracked fitting. Evren listened closely, but the fracture offered no useful echo. A hairline crack had spread across its face, so the keeper lifted it from the wall before it broke apart in the socket.

By noon, the silence had become everyone's business.

“It's empty,” said a woman carrying two cooking jars.

“It isn't,” the keeper replied. “Silence tells us the fitting is broken.”

The visiting boat crew wanted to fill their barrels before sailing. The gardeners feared their beds would die without a share. Families began counting the jars in their kitchens, and every person who passed the cistern looked into its dark mouth as if the water might confess something.

The record keeper arrived with the measuring rod tucked beneath one arm. She lowered it to the painted mark, drew it up, and checked the reading twice. Then she compared it with the old entries.

“The level has fallen,” she said. “But the cistern is not empty.”

“Can the rod tell us how much is safe to drink?” asked a child.

“No,” she said. “It tells us the level. We still have to inspect the water.”

They found a small crack in the cistern wall as well. It was not wide, but water had been escaping through it. The keeper marked the crack, and the repairers began work while the record keeper copied the last readings onto a board where everyone could see them.

They argued over the ration. Drinking and cooking came first. Gardens would receive smaller shares until the next reliable supply. The visiting crew could buy a limited reserve only after essential household allotments were set aside. The keepers would record each draw, and anyone could ask to see the tally.

The decision pleased no one completely. A gardener left the meeting with her jaw clenched. The captain said his crew might have to change its route. But the rules were visible, and the water was measured rather than guessed.

Several days later, Evren helped the keeper refit the repaired stone. The keeper tapped it once.

A thin echo answered, trembling through the stone. Its pitch was lower than the sound people remembered. The record keeper checked the rod before she wrote anything on the board.

“So the lower note means we know how much is left?” asked the gardener.

“No,” the record keeper said. “The note gives us a rough clue. The rod gives us the level at this moment. Neither tells us the exact volume we can safely use, and the sound cannot tell us whether the water is clean.”

That evening, a child tapped the stormglass and listened to the echo fade into the wind moving over the cistern mouth.

“Can it tell us when the next storm will come?”

The keeper shook his head. “It remembers a sound. It does not promise another one.”

The child put an ear against the warm stone, as if the old storm might have one more secret left. The cistern gave no answer beyond its small, fading echo.

**What this draft establishes provisionally**
- The Stormglass Echo is a maintained local magical fitting whose pitch may shift as the water level falls; it is a qualitative clue, not a calibrated measure of volume.
- Breaking the fitting removes the signal, not the water itself.
- The cistern also has an ordinary leak, and fixing it requires inspection and repair.
- Public readings and ration records help the community act, but do not remove the hardship.
- The sound is not a forecast and does not promise future rain.

**What remains open**
- How stormglass is made and why it echoes the last storm.
- How accurate its pitch is across different cisterns.
- Whether the island's next water supply comes from rain or a visiting vessel.
- Whether the visible ration board becomes a lasting local custom.

## Cross-story review

| Test | Returning Current | Climbing Thread | Stormglass Echo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magical signature | A ribbon of water runs upstream | A thread of water climbs a groove | A stone returns the sound of a past storm |
| Emotional center | Fear during a dangerous crossing | A young caretaker risks admitting what he cannot know | Anxiety when a familiar signal disappears |
| Character's distinct role | Mira acts quickly and challenges an unfair toll | Evin learns that an honest record can include uncertainty | Evren tests the fitting's sound while the record keeper makes a difficult decision legible to everyone |
| Community consequence | Repair costs and access rules | Water shares and maintenance duties | Rationing and cistern repair |
| What magic cannot do | Explain the broken rope or decide a fair toll | Measure all water or prove guilt | Measure exact volume, test purity, or predict rain |

The three stories share a principle—wonder changes what people must pay attention to—but they do not imply a shared source, mechanism, era, or larger mystery.
