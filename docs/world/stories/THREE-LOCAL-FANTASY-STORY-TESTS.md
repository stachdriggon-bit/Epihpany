# EPIPHANY — Three Local Fantasy Story Tests

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Provisional short-story drafts for testing the magic framework; not approved historical canon.  
**Purpose:** Test three different magical phenomena in grounded local stories. No global calendar dates are assigned. The drafts are not yet registered as historical events; event IDs should be added only after story approval.

## 1. Seven Breaths Beneath the Middle Arch

**Place:** IL-LOC-01 — The Three-Arch Ferry  
**Phenomenon:** MP-IL-001 — The Returning Current  
**Story focus:** Repair costs, access, and the difference between a wonder and a solution

The market had nearly closed when the ferry rope jerked in Nera's hands.

Beneath the middle arch, a narrow ribbon of water had begun to run upstream. It shone like a strip of dark glass turned against the river. The boat drifted sideways, and the sacks of flour slid across its deck.

“Hold the rope!” Nera shouted.

The ferryman braced his boots against the landing post. For seven breaths, the little current pushed against the main river. Then it vanished. The boat bumped the bank hard enough to crack a wooden crate, but no one fell into the water.

By morning, everyone at the crossing had a different account. One trader said the river had warned them. A mill worker insisted the current always appeared before a storm. The ferryman said only that the rope had been badly frayed for weeks.

The bridge keeper saw an opportunity to settle an old argument. The stone arches were expensive to inspect, and the ferry equipment needed replacing. He proposed a toll on every traveler. The ferrymen objected: the crossing had served people long before the market grew, and the poorest families crossed most often.

Nera did not know why the current had reversed. But she had seen the ferry swing when the rope slackened. She asked the bridge keeper to inspect the anchor points before anyone argued about the river's intentions.

The inspection found a worn ring and a split timber brace. Neither explained the strange current, but both made another accident more likely. The community agreed to a temporary repair levy on loaded commercial carts, while people on foot could still cross without paying. They would review the arrangement after the repairs and publish the costs at the market.

The next evening, the river flowed in its ordinary direction. Nera watched the middle arch anyway.

The current had given them a reason to look closely. It had not told them what a fair toll should be.

**What this draft establishes provisionally**
- The Returning Current can create a real, visible hazard at the middle arch.
- Local witnesses interpret the event differently.
- Ordinary maintenance still matters; the magical event does not explain the damaged rope or settle the toll dispute.

**What remains open**
- Why and how often the current appears.
- Whether the temporary levy survives.
- Any exact date, origin story, or connection to other phenomena.

## 2. The Thread That Climbed

**Place:** TH-LOC-02 — The Spring Steps  
**Phenomenon:** MP-TH-001 — The Climbing Thread  
**Story focus:** Water-sharing, evidence, and avoiding blame

For three weeks, the upper terraces had received less water than the gardeners expected. The channels still glimmered after sunrise, but the flow no longer reached the last beds before midday.

At dusk, Sami, the youngest channel caretaker, knelt beside the lowest carved step. The Climbing Thread sometimes appeared there: a line of water as thin as a hair, rising up the groove for a few moments before slipping back down.

That evening, it rose only to the second mark, then disappeared.

“Someone has taken more than their share,” said a gardener whose terrace had gone dry. “The thread used to climb higher.”

Sami wanted to believe him. The maintenance rota had been ignored twice, and the same households seemed always to be repairing the channels. But the thread had never been a measuring cup, and no one had recorded exactly how far it climbed on earlier evenings.

Sami and an older repairer followed the channel by lantern light. Near the third terrace, they found a mat of roots and silt narrowing a side passage. It was a real obstruction. They cleared it, marked the damaged stone, and wrote down what they had found.

The next evening, the thread climbed a little farther. The upper beds still did not receive enough water. Clearing one passage had helped, but the spring itself was weaker than it had been, and no one knew whether the cause was seasonal, upstream, or something else.

At the meeting, the gardeners agreed on two steps. First, they would repair the cracked channel and keep a daily record of flow at the same points. Second, until they understood the shortage better, each terrace would receive a rotating share, with the schedule posted where everyone could read it. The households that had done the most recent repairs would not be expected to do every job again.

Sami returned at dusk and watched the thread rise along the groove. It stopped before the highest mark.

This time, nobody accused a neighbor. They wrote down what they had seen and left the question open.

**What this draft establishes provisionally**
- The Climbing Thread is a small local wonder, not a source of extra water.
- It can prompt inspection but cannot diagnose every problem or prove wrongdoing.
- A practical repair and fairer rota help, while the spring's reduced flow remains unexplained.

**What remains open**
- The phenomenon's conditions and cause.
- The reason the spring flow weakened.
- Whether the community's new sharing schedule lasts.

## 3. When the Cistern Fell Silent

**Place:** AZ-LOC-02 — The Rain-Cistern Island  
**Phenomenon:** MP-AZ-001 — The Stormglass Echo  
**Story focus:** Maintenance, uncertainty, and fair rationing

The island's oldest cistern had a sound that everyone knew. When a keeper tapped the small stone set into its wall, the cistern answered with a faint echo of the storm that had last filled it. As the water sank lower, the echo changed pitch.

The keepers called the fitting stormglass, although it was grey stone, rough at the edges and no larger than a thumb.

One morning, the sound stopped.

A crack had spread across the fitting. The cistern keeper removed it before the fracture widened, and the water answered no more. By noon, people were already arguing. One family said the silence meant the cistern was nearly empty. A visiting boat crew said the water should be reserved for residents first. The gardeners worried that without a trustworthy signal, they would lose their plants before the next rain.

The record keeper brought out the measuring rod. They lowered it at the marked place, checked the reading twice, and compared it with the previous entries. The cistern held less water than it had a month earlier, but it was not empty. The rod could measure the level; it could not tell them whether the water was clean, nor when rain would come.

They repaired a small crack in the cistern wall and rationed water by written daily allotments. Drinking and cooking came first; gardens received reduced shares, and visiting crews could buy a limited reserve only after the essential household allotments were set. The rules were posted publicly, and the keepers recorded each draw.

The stormglass fitting took several days to mend. When it was set back into the wall, the next gentle tap brought a thin echo, lower than before. The keepers compared it with the rod instead of treating it as an exact measure.

That evening, a child tapped the stone and asked whether it could tell them when the next storm would arrive.

“No,” the keeper said. “It remembers a sound. It does not promise another one.”

The child listened to the echo until it faded into the island wind.

**What this draft establishes provisionally**
- The Stormglass Echo is a maintained local tool that gives an approximate water-level signal.
- A broken fitting removes the signal, not the water itself.
- Direct measurements and public rationing remain necessary.

**What remains open**
- How stormglass is made and why it echoes the last storm.
- How accurate its pitch is across different cisterns.
- Whether the island's next water supply comes from rain or a visiting vessel.
